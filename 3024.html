Section 3024. Most people skimmed past it. The media called it a "waiver for healthcare regulations." But in legal circles, it has been called the "Holy Grail of Control" by those who interpret it broadly.

📖 Original Text (paraphrased): The Secretary of Health and Human Services may waive certain provisions of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and the Public Health Service Act during a public health emergency, including requirements related to informed consent for certain research.

Critics argue: This waiver, combined with the "minimal risk" definition, creates a loophole that could permit research without informed consent.

📖 THE "NORMAL LIFE" LOOPHOLE (AS INTERPRETED BY CRITICS)

The interpretation that concerns civil liberties advocates:

Federal law states that research is permissible without informed consent if the risk involved is "not greater than minimal risk."

"Minimal risk" is defined as: the probability and magnitude of harm or discomfort not greater than those ordinarily encountered in daily life.

The concern: Death happens in daily life. Heart attacks, stroke, cancer, and sudden death are "ordinarily encountered" realities of human existence.

Critics argue that this creates a logical trap: if death is statistically normal, then inducing death could be classified as "minimal risk" — making non-consensual experimentation potentially legal under a broad reading of the statute.

Important Note: This interpretation is vigorously debated. No court has adopted it. The FDA maintains that informed consent requirements remain in effect. The concern is that the statutory language, if interpreted literally and broadly, creates a dangerous ambiguity.

🔍 THE FOIA BLACK HOLE

Since the passage of Section 3024, transparency advocates have attempted to obtain records related to research conducted under the waiver. Many report receiving:

  • 1. "Glomar Response": "We can neither confirm nor deny the existence of such records."
  • 2. National Security Exemption: "Disclosure would damage national security."
  • 3. Administrative Burdens: Extensive delays and fees.

💡 Documented FOIA Cases: Multiple nonprofit organizations have filed FOIA requests for records of research conducted under Section 3024. Response rates: less than 5% within statutory deadlines. Most receive the "Glomar" response — neither confirming nor denying the existence of the requested records.
