The Word “Person”

A field tool for the reader who wants to check the record — not to be told what to believe. Name the specific. Find the corroboration. Mark the gap. Refuse to overclaim.

Four steps. Non-negotiable.

This is the same method the book uses in every chapter. The app does not do the thinking for you. It holds the structure so you can do it yourself.

1
Name the specific. Not “the law hides things.” The statute, the case, the date, the exact sentence. Vague claims cannot be checked. Specific ones can.
2
Find the corroboration. What does the primary record actually say? Not the article about the record. Not the video about the record. The record.
3
Mark the gap. What is unknown? What has not been tested in court? What has not been independently verified? Say it plainly.
4
Refuse to overclaim. If the record does not support the claim, say so. Do not soften it. Do not hedge it into meaninglessness. State what the record supports — and stop there.

What this chapter is actually about

The word “person” in American law has a history. It is not a synonym for “human being.” Federal law defines it to include corporations. The 14th Amendment created a category of United States citizenship distinct from state citizenship, and the Supreme Court said so in The Slaughter-House Cases (1873). Those are documented facts.

What follows from those facts is where people disagree — often violently. This app does not resolve the disagreement. It shows you where the record ends and interpretation begins.

The Record — primary sources

These are the documents the chapter stands on. Read them yourself. Do not take this app's word for it.

The statute · primary

1 U.S.C. § 1 — The Dictionary Act. The federal definition of “person.”

The words “person” and “whoever” include corporations, companies, associations, firms, partnerships, societies, and joint stock companies, as well as individuals.

Read the statute — Cornell LII

The case · primary

Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. 393 (1857). Taney's distinction between “the people of the United States” and “persons.” The case's racism is repudiated; its structural distinction was not erased by the 14th Amendment — it was worked around. Read the language yourself and note what it does and does not say.

Read the opinion — Justia

The case · primary

The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36 (1873). The Court's holding that United States citizenship and state citizenship are distinct, and that the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the 14th Amendment protects only the former. This is the hinge on which much of the “person vs. people” argument turns. Read what the Court actually wrote.

Read the opinion — Justia

The amendment · primary

U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. “All persons born or naturalized in the United States... are citizens of the United States and of the State wherein they reside.” Note the word: persons. Read it against the two cases above.

Read the amendment — National Archives

The secondary source · contested

“A Treatise on the Word ‘Person’” — Williams and Williams Law Firm. The long argument the chapter engages. It has a point of view. It cites real cases. It also interprets them through a specific lens. Read it as an argument, not as the record.

Read the Treatise

How to read a case for yourself

  • Find the holding — the sentence that begins “We hold that...”
  • Separate holding from dicta — what the court decided vs. what it mused about
  • Note what the court did not decide
  • Check whether a later case overruled or narrowed it
  • Ask: does the Treatise's quote match the sentence in context?

The Gaps — stated plainly

The chapter's honesty lives here. These are the places where the record ends and interpretation begins. Mark them. Do not paper over them.

Gap · scope

“Person” means different things in different statutes. The Dictionary Act sets a default for Acts of Congress, but courts construe “person” contextually. A claim that “person always means X” is not supported. A claim that “person can include corporations in federal law” is supported.

Gap · consequences

The record does not support the leap from “person includes corporations” to “you are a corporation.” The Treatise sometimes moves from the first to the second. The first is documented. The second is an interpretation, and it is contested. Mark it.

Gap · remedy

The record does not support “VOID — NO CONTRACT” filings as a legal remedy. Courts have consistently rejected sovereign-citizen theories. This is not a guardrail — it is what the case law shows. Telling a reader otherwise can get them jailed. The chapter must not do that, and this app will not either.

Gap · the 14th Amendment

“Circumvented, not overturned” is a contested reading. It is an argument some scholars and practitioners make. It is not the settled holding of any court. The chapter can present it as an argument and mark it as such. It cannot present it as established fact.

Gap · what is unknown

No court has ever held that the word “person” in ordinary legal usage strips a human being of constitutional rights. If a reader encounters that claim, the burden is on the claim to produce the case. The app has not found one. Mark the gap.

The Question Kit

Carry these. They work on any claim — from any side. The point is not to win. The point is to find the step where the claim stops being evidence and starts being belief.

Five questions

1
What is the specific?Which statute, case, date, sentence? If the claim cannot be made specific, it cannot be checked.
2
What is the primary source?The record itself — not the article about it. Read the case. Read the statute. Read the amendment.
3
Where is the step?Find the sentence where the claim moves from “this is documented” to “therefore this follows.” That is the place to stop.
4
What would prove this wrong?If nothing would, it is not a claim about the record. It is a belief. Both can matter. They are not the same thing.
5
Who benefits from me believing this without checking?Ask it of every claim — including the ones you like. Especially the ones you like.

The oath of the reader

“I will not believe a thing because it makes me feel like I am part of a brave minority. I will not reject a thing because it makes me uncomfortable. I will check. I will mark the gap. I will refuse to overclaim. And when the record does not support what I want it to support, I will say so — plainly.”
