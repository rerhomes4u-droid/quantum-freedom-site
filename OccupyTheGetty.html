Case Study · Media Literacy

“The Children Under the Getty”

How one claim was made, spread, and tested — and what the test found.

Step 01 — The Claim

What is being asserted

The “Occupy the Getty” campaign asserts that a network of tunnels and underground bunkers beneath the Getty Museum in Los Angeles is used for child trafficking. The claim’s originator is Steven D. Kelley, who describes himself as a former CIA/NSA contractor and laser optics engineer.

The core assertion, as stated by its source

“Underneath the Getty Museum is a network of tunnels and underground bunkers according to former CIA/NSA contractor STEVEN D. KELLEY. Child trafficking hub.”

— The campaign’s own public description

Step 02 — The Sources

Where the claim lives

These are the primary locations of the campaign, included so the reader can examine them directly. Note what each one is: a social media account, a personal website, a self-published book, a video platform. None is an investigation, a court filing, or a news report.

  • x.com/occupythegetty The campaign’s social media account. Posts are assertions and video clips; no documentary evidence is posted.
  • stevendkelley.com Kelley’s personal site, where he sells his self-published book and promotes the campaign.
  • childrenunderthegetty.com — “What is Occupy the Getty?” The campaign’s own explainer. It states Kelley “was informed of the areas under the Getty Museum by its Trust Fund Manager.” It offers no document, no name, no date, and no record of that conversation.
  • rumble.com/c/StevenDKelley Kelley’s video channel. (The page may return an error in some browsers; the channel exists and is linked from the campaign.)
  • brighteon.com — “Getty Occupy The Getty” A campaign video. The description lists a Telegram channel and a set of coordinates, and states: “Look 1+ Mile Below the Super Structure for yourself.”
Step 03 — The Test

What happened when journalists asked for proof

This is the decisive part of the case. Independent fact-checkers did not merely disagree with the claim. They contacted its source and asked him to support it. Here is what they found.

The claim

Children are trafficked through tunnels beneath the Getty Museum.

What the check found

USA Today asked Kelley directly for proof. He provided a PDF of his 2011 e-book — which claims he learned of the underground facility through a psychic who visited it via “astral projection.” The book does not mention children under the Getty.

The claim

A rescue operation was conducted; the tunnels were cleared.

What the check found

AFP Factual found no record of any rescue. The Getty’s communications vice president called the claims “totally false and fictional.” PolitiFact rated the claim “Pants on Fire” and reported that the LAPD had no information about any such investigation.

Independent rating PANTS ON FIRE — FALSE

The claim’s own originator, when asked for evidence, supplied a book that attributes his knowledge to a psychic’s astral projection. No document, witness, rescue record, or official investigation supports the trafficking assertion.

Step 04 — The Question You Asked

So why does it feel suppressed?

This is a fair question, and it deserves a real answer. The campaign has not been silenced — its accounts, videos, and website are all publicly reachable, and are linked above. What has happened is different: it has been fact-checked and found baseless, and platforms have sometimes limited its reach for that reason.

“Broken links” and “taken-down videos” are real phenomena — but they happen to debunked content for the ordinary reason that platforms act on reports of false claims. That is not the same as a coordinated cover-up, and it is not evidence that the claim is true. The test of a claim is the evidence offered for it. Here, the evidence offered was astral projection.

The question “why can we write fiction about anything, but not question this?” has a simple answer: you can question it — and the questioning was done. The result is above. Fiction is understood as fiction. A factual accusation stands or falls on its evidence, and this one fell when its source was asked to produce it.

Step 05 — The Tool

How to test the next claim you meet

Use this on any claim — this one, or one you encounter tomorrow. It is the same method the fact-checkers used.

  1. Find the origin. Who first asserted this? Not the account that shared it — the person or document it traces back to.
  2. Ask the origin for evidence. A document, a named witness, a court record, a recording. If the origin, asked directly, offers a book, a feeling, or a psychic — you have your answer.
  3. Distinguish assertion from evidence. “They say” is not a document. A video of someone claiming a thing is not a record of the thing.
  4. Look for what a real investigation would leave. Arrests, filings, named officials, dates, case numbers. A claim with none of these, after years, is not being suppressed — it is unsupported.
  5. Check whether the claim has been tested. Search “[claim] fact check.” If independent outlets contacted the source and the source produced nothing, that is the result of the test.
  6. Notice when “censorship” is doing the work. “They’re silencing this” can be true or false — but it is never evidence that the underlying claim is correct. It is a separate question, and it is answerable.
