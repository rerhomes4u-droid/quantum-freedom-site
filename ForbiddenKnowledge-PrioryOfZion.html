01 The Official Record: A Hoax, Admitted Under Oath

Official position — stated fairly, and it is devastating

The Priory of Sion was registered with the French government on May 7, 1956, in Annemasse, by Pierre Plantard, André Bonhomme, Jean Delaval, and Armand Defago. The French National Library catalog entry states plainly that Plantard "sought to make believe that this association had secretly existed since the Middle Ages" [citation:15].

The "Dossiers Secrets" were fabricated by Plantard and Philippe de Chérisey and deposited in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. The grand masters list — Leonardo, Newton, Victor Hugo, Botticelli, Debussy — was fabricated [citation:3][citation:10].

In September 1993, Plantard admitted the hoax under oath. A French judge investigating a corruption scandal involving Roger-Patrice Pelat (whom Plantard had claimed was a grand master) ordered a search of Plantard's home. Under oath, Plantard admitted he had invented everything. He received a severe warning and was told not to "play" with the French judicial system [citation:1].

In 1996, André Bonhomme told the BBC: "We were four friends who got together to have fun. We called ourselves the Priory of Sion because there was a mountain nearby with the same name. I haven't seen Pierre Plantard in over twenty years... but he always had a great imagination" [citation:1][citation:3].

On the specific claim that the Priory of Sion was an ancient secret society protecting a Merovingian–Jesus bloodline: the record is closed. It was a 20th-century fabrication. The fabricator admitted it. His co-founder laughed about it on the record.

03 The One Piece of Hard Science — And Its Limit

In 2006, the History Channel program *Digging for the Truth* ran a DNA test on Arnegonde, the fifth wife of the Merovingian king Clotaire I. The result: her DNA "did not have the markers that might be expected from Middle Eastern populations; instead, her DNA showed she came from Europe" . The program presented this as evidence against the Jesus–Magdalene bloodline claim.

The gap: the DNA test tested the wrong person

Arnegonde was a wife who married into the Merovingian dynasty. She was not a blood descendant of Merovech. The Merovingian line passed through the male line. Testing the wife of a Merovingian king tells you about *her* ancestry, not about the dynasty's Y-chromosome.

The Wikipedia discussion page on the Merovingian dynasty includes a detailed critique of this exact point: the test was performed on "the wife of a Merovingian, not a Merovingian." The program itself, according to that discussion, acknowledged the limits of what the test could prove .

The official debunking used a DNA test that could not answer the question it claimed to answer. That does not make the bloodline claim true. It makes the refutation weaker than it is presented.

04 The "Sea Creature" Claim: Real as a Myth, Not as History

The Merovingians did claim descent from a sea beast. Fredegar, writing around 660, recorded that Chlodio's wife was impregnated by a "beast of Neptune rather like a Quinotaur" while bathing in the sea, and gave birth to Merovech [citation:13].

Modern scholarship treats this as a foundation myth — a claim the Merovingians made about themselves, not a historical event [citation:6]. The "sea creature" detail is real as a *claim*. It is not evidence of literal descent from a sea creature. The gap here is not evidentiary. It is interpretive: the Merovingians claimed divine/sacral kingship. What that meant to them is a historical question. What it means for a 21st-century bloodline claim is a different question, and the record does not bridge it.

05 Testing the Claims — The Four Steps

Claim: The Priory of Sion was an ancient secret society

Disputed → Record proves fabrication

Name the specific: That the Priory of Sion existed before 1956 and protected a Merovingian–Jesus bloodline.

Corroboration: The French National Library catalog confirms registration on May 7, 1956 [citation:15]. Plantard admitted the hoax under oath in 1993 [citation:1]. Bonhomme confirmed it to the BBC in 1996 [citation:1][citation:3]. The Dossiers Secrets are fabricated [citation:3].

Gap: None. The record is closed.

Refuse to overclaim: The record proves this claim is false. It was a 20th-century hoax.

Claim: The Merovingians descend from Jesus and Mary Magdalene

Unverified → No documentary support, no valid genetic refutation

Name the specific: That a verifiable bloodline connects Jesus of Nazareth and Mary Magdalene to the Merovingian kings of Francia.

Corroboration: No documentary record from any period before the 20th century supports this. The Priory documents that "proved" it are fabricated [citation:3]. The books that popularized it (Baigent/Leigh/Lincoln, Gardner, Church) are not academic histories [citation:18].

Gap: The DNA test that supposedly refuted it tested Arnegonde, a wife who married into the dynasty, not a blood descendant . The claim is unproven. It is not disproven by the genetic evidence that has been presented.

Refuse to overclaim: The record does not support the claim. The record also does not contain a valid genetic refutation. The honest verdict is: no documentary support exists, and the official refutation is weaker than it is presented.

Claim: The Merovingians claimed descent from a sea creature

Verified — as a historical claim they made about themselves

Name the specific: That Merovingian foundation mythology included descent from a Quinotaur, a sea beast.

Corroboration: Fredegar, writing c. 660, recorded the Quinotaur story [citation:13]. Modern scholarship accepts that the Merovingians held a sacral kingship ideology involving divine descent [citation:6].

Gap: The gap is interpretive, not evidentiary. What the myth meant to the Merovingians is a historical question. What it means for modern bloodline claims is not answered by the record.

Refuse to overclaim: The record supports the existence of the myth. It does not support literal descent from a sea creature.

06 For the Reader Who Wants the Bloodline to Be True

The Priory of Sion is not your evidence. It was a hoax, admitted by the hoaxer. If you build your case on Plantard's documents, you are building on sand, and the official side will bury you with Plantard's own confession.

But the *question* the hoax attached itself to is older than Plantard and survives him. Did Jesus have a bloodline? Did it survive? The record does not answer that question. The record answers a different question: did Pierre Plantard invent a secret society to prove it? Yes. He did. Under oath, he said so.

That is not the same as disproving the bloodline. It is disproving the *vehicle* that claimed to prove it. The distinction matters. Hold the question. Drop the hoax.
