The record is clear. The Documented Plants of Burkina Faso

These are not the "70 cures of Traore." That specific list is not in the record. What is in the record is the documented traditional pharmacopoeia of Burkina Faso—the plants that healers have used for generations, the plants that Sankara's government tried to integrate into the public health system, the plants that are still used today. Each entry below is sourced from ethnobotanical and pharmacological studies.

Read this before you use any plant: This is not medical advice. Some of these plants are toxic in high doses. Some interact with pharmaceutical drugs. Some are dangerous for pregnant women. The record documents traditional use. It does not prescribe. If you are sick, see a doctor. If you want to use plants, work with a qualified herbalist or a traditional healer who knows the plant, the dose, and the contraindications. The goal is not to replace medicine. Cochlospermum planchonii (N'Dribala)

Used for: Malaria, fever, jaundice

This low shrub is common in West Africa. A decoction of the rhizome is traditionally used to treat fevers and malaria in Burkina Faso, Ivory Coast, Niger, and Nigeria [citation:10]. The roots were the most effective part in laboratory tests. A clinical study at Banfora Hospital in Burkina Faso in 1995–1997 compared the plant to chloroquine. Both treatments produced statistically similar parasite clearance rates. In 2005, the Ministry of Health officially licensed N'Dribala as an antimalarial phytomedicine [citation:10].

Source: "SAYE: The Story of an Antimalarial Phytomedicine from Burkina Faso," Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine.

Guiera senegalensis

Used for: A wide range of ailments, including malaria, dysentery, diarrhea, cough, abdominal pain, hypertension, diabetes, hemorrhoids, eczema, and more

Regarded by traditional health practitioners of Burkina Faso, Senegal, and Mali as a "miracle remedy" [citation:22]. The leaves contain alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and saponins. Studies have shown antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimalarial properties. The plant is one of the most cited in ethnobotanical surveys in the region.

Source: "Antioxidant, Anti-inflammatory, and Cytotoxicity Properties of Five Medicinal Plants Used by Traditional Healers in Burkina Faso," International Journal of Biochemistry Research & Review.

Euphorbia hirta

Used for: The most utilized species in the Central-West region, with the highest use value and citation frequency

Ethnobotanical studies in the Central-West region of Burkina Faso found that Euphorbia hirta had the highest use value (0.737) and the highest relative frequency of citation (0.15) among the plants studied [citation:8]. It is used for multiple therapeutic applications. The plant is well-established in the local pharmacopoeia.

Source: "Medicinal Plant Diversity and Traditional Knowledge Among Ethnic Groups in Burkina Faso Central-West Region," Journal of Plant Biota.

Annona senegalensis

Used for: Snakebite, fever, and other ailments

A study of traditional antivenom treatments in Burkina Faso found that Annona senegalensis had the highest relative frequency of citation (RFC=10%) among 29 plants used by traditional health practitioners for snakebite envenomation [citation:11]. The study noted that 63.3% of the traditional practitioners had received formal training and 46.7% would refer patients to formal healthcare when treatment failed—evidence of a collaborative model that Sankara's government tried to formalize.

Source: "Ethnobotanical study of traditional antivenom treatments in Burkina Faso," Tropical Medicine and Health (2025).

The ABC Burkina Pharmacopoeia

A documented collection of traditional recipes from Burkina Faso

The ABC Burkina project has documented dozens of traditional recipes, including treatments for fever and malaria using Securidaca longipedunculata, liver and kidney decongestants using Combretum micranthum (kinkéliba), treatments for hemorrhoids, hernias, and hemorrhages [citation:5]. The recipes include the plant name, the local name in Mooré, Lyélé, or Nuni, the part used, the preparation method, and the dosage. The document is a public record of the traditional pharmacopoeia.

Source: ABC Burkina, "Pharmacopée traditionnelle."

SAYE (the combination)

Used for: Jaundice and malaria

Developed by Dr. Zephirin Dakuvo in Burkina Faso, SAYE combines Cochlospermum planchonii, Phyllanthus amarus, and Cassia alata. It was developed for hepatitis but patients reported it was also effective for malaria. It was officially licensed as an antimalarial phytomedicine in Burkina Faso in 2005 [citation:10].

Source: "SAYE: The Story of an Antimalarial Phytomedicine from Burkina Faso."

The full pharmacopoeia is much larger. The Sanan people of northwest Burkina Faso use 94 plant species for different pathologies [citation:9]. The Ouagadougou markets document 42 species [citation:6]. The liver disease study documented 139 plant species with secondary medicinal applications [citation:16]. This is a living tradition, not a dead list. It is the medicine that Sankara tried to bring into the light.
