Point by Point

Each card below states a claim the notice makes, then what actually happens when it meets a real court. The pattern is consistent: the argument has been tried, and it has failed.

Claim 01 — Statutes don't apply to non-employees

“If you’re not employed by that corporation … they simply do not apply to you.”

What courts do: Reject it. Statutes apply to persons within the jurisdiction, not only to government employees. This is the core “sovereign citizen” premise, and it has been rejected in every federal circuit that has addressed it.

Courts have described the underlying theories as “incoherent,” “unintelligible,” and “patently frivolous.” A California federal court dismissed a suit built on these arguments with prejudice.

Claim 02 — “Person” means federal employee

“That nasty little word ‘person’ … is actually code for federal employee.”

What courts do: Reject it. “Person” is defined by statute and interpreted by courts to include individuals, corporations, and other entities. No court has held that “person” in the U.S. Code means only federal employees.

This is a hallmark of pseudolaw: redefining ordinary legal terms to create a parallel legal universe.

Claim 03 — County court is superior to the Supreme Court

“Your local county courthouse … is superior to their Supreme Court.”

What courts do: The Seventh Amendment protects jury findings from re-examination, but it does not make a county court superior to the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court is the final appellate authority in the federal system.

The notice reads the Seventh Amendment far beyond its text and settled precedent.

Claim 04 — 83 cases vanished from PACER

“This very notice has made 83 cases vanish from PACER.”

What actually happens: No verifiable evidence exists. Cases are dismissed for ordinary reasons — lack of evidence, procedural defects, plea agreements — and the notice gets the credit. This is survivorship bias, not proof.

If a document could erase federal filings, it would be the most consequential legal discovery in American history. It isn’t.

Claim 05 — The plaintiff must appear and be cross-examined

“Require the State of Texas to show up in court … present photo ID … verify his/her claim.”

What courts do: The Sixth Amendment guarantees confrontation of witnesses against you. It does not require the prosecuting entity to appear as a physical person. The State is represented by counsel, and that is legally sufficient.

Demanding that “the State” take the stand is treated as frivolous and can draw sanctions.

Claim 06 — Filing this notice creates liability for the prosecutor

“He knows he’s about to bear the full liability … that is what makes cases disappear.”

What actually happens: Prosecutors have absolute immunity for acts within their prosecutorial function. Filing a notice does not strip that immunity. It does, however, put you on the record as making a frivolous filing.

Absolute prosecutorial immunity is longstanding Supreme Court doctrine.

Claim 07 — The fee schedule ($25,752.83 + $147.60/min)

“I charge $25,752.83 … and an additional $147.60 per minute.”

What courts do: Ignore it. You cannot unilaterally bill a court or prosecutor into liability by writing a number on a document. No court has enforced these “fee schedules.”

Unilateral “contracts” imposed on government actors are not enforceable.

Claim 08 — Call it “property,” never “child”

“The state has no control over your property … use the word ‘property,’ not ‘children.’”

What courts do: Children are not property. CPS cases are governed by child-welfare statutes, and courts apply the best-interests-of-the-child standard. Relabeling a child as “property” does not change the legal analysis and can harm your credibility in a custody proceeding.

This is one of the most dangerous claims in the document for parents facing CPS.

Claim 09 — Hand-write it, keep the original, file a copy

“Hand-write the notice … enter a copy, not the original.”

What courts do: Courts have filing rules. A handwritten document with no legal basis is still a frivolous filing. Keeping the “original” confers no legal protection.

Procedure matters in court — but procedure cannot rescue a claim with no legal foundation.

Claim 10 — Common law means “do anything you please”

“You can do anything you damn well please … as long as you’re not harming your fellow man.”

What courts do: Common law is judge-made law built from centuries of precedent — not a license for unlimited conduct. It coexists with statutes, which are also law. The notice’s version of “common law” is a fiction.

The “do no harm” formulation is a slogan, not a legal standard.

Equity

Some teachers point away from the notice and toward “equity” — a tradition of conscience, fairness, and moral suasion. It is worth understanding on its own terms. It is also not a legal cheat code, and this tab says so plainly.

This section presents equity as a rhetorical and spiritual framework. It is not legal advice, and no court has validated it as a method for defeating prosecution.

What the equity tradition claims

In the materials associated with Julian Swan and “Standing on the Rock,” equity is described as a practice of asking questions that prick the conscience — using “soft power that compels performance rather than the contention that causes people to resist.” The idea is that moral clarity, patience, and well-formed questions can move a person in authority to do what is right, where confrontation would only harden them.

Why it resonates

It resonates because it names something true: institutions often respond to moral pressure in ways they do not respond to legal technicality. A prosecutor who will not read your motion may still be moved by a victim’s family. A clerk who will not bend a rule may still be shamed by public attention. Equity, in this sense, is a real force in human affairs.

Where the claim overreaches

The overreach is the leap from “moral suasion can matter” to “this is an absolute way to win.” Courts decide cases on law and evidence. A conscience-pricking question is not a legal argument, and a judge who is not persuaded by it has not violated any rule. The equity tradition’s own teachers describe it as soft power, not as a remedy — and that distinction matters.

What equity can honestly do

It can help you speak clearly and humanely. It can help you preserve relationships that litigation destroys. It can help you tell your story in a way people remember. Those are real goods. They are not the same as a defense, and no one should walk into a courtroom believing they are.

Honest bottom line. Equity is a moral and rhetorical practice with real value. It is not a substitute for legal counsel, and it has not been validated by any court as a way to defeat a prosecution.
