📡 PACER – Public Access to Court Electronic Records

PACER gives you access to every federal court case in the United States. Cost: $0.10 per page. If you spend less than $30 per quarter (300 pages), it’s FREE. You don’t even need to enter a credit card unless you exceed $30.
🧠 Account types:
- Case Search Only (95% of users) – view and download documents.
- Filer (attorney/non-attorney) – for filing documents into a case (requires court approval).
“Sign up for Case Search Only first. You can always upgrade later.”

🔍 Searching PACER – By Court, Query, or Case Number

“You select the district (e.g., Central District of California), log in, then use Query to search by case number, party name, or cause of action. The interface looks like 1995 – that’s fine. It works.”

Wesley Snipes case example: “USA vs Snipes et al. We found the docket, 602 filings deep. You can download the original indictment for $0.40. Every filing is public. This is the TV channel of litigation – pay‑per‑view to watch the fight.”

📌 “When you search, the system may cut off the case number. Just type it, hit ‘Run Query.’ It will find it.”

📋 The Docket – The Timeline of the War

“The docket is a list of every single filing in the case – complaints, motions, oppositions, replies, orders. Each item has a number (1, 2, 3…). Click any number to view/download the document.”
📊 Brandon’s AMX case docket: 28 filings deep as of this recording. The train hasn’t even left the station. The judge hasn’t signed a single order yet – only procedural filings.
🎯 “When you open a docket, you see all the punches and counter-punches. Every motion to dismiss, every opposition, every reply. This is the boxing match. Watch it live.”

✍️ Filing Into a Case – Pro Se Electronic Filing

“If you are the plaintiff (or a party), you can request electronic filing privileges. The court will approve it (they approved mine). Then you log in, go to ‘Civil’ → ‘Motion’ or ‘Other Filing,’ select the filing type, upload your document, and submit.”

What you can file: motions to strike, oppositions, replies, notices, service of process affidavits, proposed orders.
Cost: The filing fee for a new case is $45 (federal). There is no per‑motion fee.

🏓 The Ping‑Pong of Litigation (1‑2‑3)

Round 1: Plaintiff files motion (e.g., motion to strike).
Round 2: Defendant files opposition (response).
Round 3: Plaintiff files reply to the opposition.
Then the court decides. Each party gets up to bat twice. The initiator has the advantage (two swings).
💣 “In the AMX case, we have multiple overlapping ping‑pongs – motion to dismiss, motion for relief from local rule, motion to strike – all happening simultaneously. That’s how you get 28 filings before the judge has said a word.”

📜 Local Rules – They Are Law (Not Suggestions)

“Local rules (e.g., Central District of California Local Rule 7-3) are mandatory. They require a meet and confer – preferably in person – before filing most motions. If an attorney violates local rules, they are violating the law.”

Example – Local Rule 7-3: “Any motion must first contact opposing counsel to discuss thoroughly, preferably in person, the substance of the contemplated motion and any potential resolution.”

🔥 “The defense attorney in the AMX case filed a motion to dismiss without a meet and confer. Then she asked for ‘relief from local rule 7-3’ – meaning she wanted an exception. That’s a sign of a weak case. I opposed it and cited the rule. The judge hasn’t ruled yet.”

🤝 The Meet and Confer – Don’t Be Scared

“Local Rule 7-3 requires a meet and confer before motions. I offered to meet in person (the rule says ‘preferably in person’). The defense attorney refused, canceled the scheduled call, and demanded all future communications in writing – which is not a meet and confer.”
🧠 “They are terrified of meeting you in person. Because in person, you are calm, educated, and certain. On paper, they can twist your words. But the rule requires a meet and confer. If they refuse, file a motion to strike their motion for failure to comply with local rules.”

⚖️ Declarations vs. Affidavits – Why They Avoid Perjury

“An affidavit is sworn under penalty of perjury. If you prove it’s false, they go to prison.
A declaration is not under penalty of perjury (or is under a weaker standard). Defense attorneys file DECLARATIONS, not affidavits, because they don’t want to risk perjury.”

“In the AMX case, every filing from the defense is a declaration – not one affidavit. That tells you something. They know they are walking a fine line.”

💰 Irrelevant Billables – The Defense’s Tactic

“In their motion for relief from local rule 7-3, the defense attorney attached 14 exhibits – copies of my website, my about page, my Instagram, even my humor. None of it was relevant to the motion. They were running up billable hours and trying to embarrass me.”
🎯 “Objection: Irrelevant. But you must still respond. Acknowledge it’s irrelevant, then move on. Never ignore anything they file – because if you don’t oppose it, they’ll argue you conceded.”

📧 The Settlement Demand – “Threats” vs. Transparency

“I sent a settlement demand: waive 154 and 155 (refund of payments + $250M) if they give me a Centurion Black Card with no limit (156). I also disclosed that I was researching criminal indictments – not a threat, just transparency under local rule 7-3.”

The defense’s reaction: They called it a threat, canceled the meet and confer, and demanded all future communication in writing. Then they filed a motion for relief from local rule 7-3 based on ‘fear.’
“They twisted my words to get billables. It’s fine. I opposed it. Now we wait.”
