▣ I. THE FILTER · THE DOUBLE STANDARD

The "Share a Coke" Scandal

In August 2026, users discovered that Coca-Cola's online personalization tool—which allows customers to print custom names and phrases on cans—had a glaring double standard. The system allowed phrases like "Satan is Good" and "Jesus is the Devil" to pass the preview filter, while blocking "Jesus is King," "Jesus is Good," "Virgin Mary," and "Christ is King" [citation:3][citation:4].

Fox News Digital conducted a subsequent investigation, finding that "Allah is Lord" was also blocked, while "God is Dead," "Atheist Pride," "Shalom," and "Buddha Bless" were allowed [citation:11].

Coca-Cola responded by calling it a "technical issue" and temporarily disabled the preview feature, stating that "religious, political, trademarked, inappropriate, or otherwise restricted terms" are subject to moderation [citation:3].

▣ II. THE WATER · LA CALERA, COLOMBIA

The Springs Covered in Cement

In September 2025, Colombian Congresswoman María del Mar Pizarro denounced that Coca-Cola (through its bottler INDEGA) had covered seven natural springs in La Calera, Cundinamarca, with cement and metal sheets [citation:13].

The company denied "blocking" the springs, claiming it installed "protection covers" for sanitary reasons. However, a 2023 technical report from the regional environmental authority (CAR) had already found inconsistencies between Coca-Cola's consumption reports and actual figures [citation:13].

In February 2024, the municipality ordered water rationing due to reduced flow—while the Coca-Cola plant continued bottling water for commercial sale [citation:13].

Source: La Opinión (Colombia), El Espectador (April 2026), Congressional denunciation by Rep. María del Mar Pizarro.

The CAR Ruling: A Partial Victory

In April 2026, after public pressure, the CAR ruled on Coca-Cola's concession. The company lost three of its seven springs, its extraction rate was cut by 42% (from 3.23 to 1.9 liters per second), and its permit was reduced from 10 years to 5 years [citation:5].

The CAR director stated the decision was based on "technical analysis" and that water must be prioritized for human consumption [citation:5].

▣ III. THE BLOOD · THE UNIONISTS

The Sinaltrainal Case

In 2001, the United Steelworkers and the International Labor Rights Fund sued Coca-Cola and its bottlers in Miami federal court on behalf of Sinaltrainal, the Colombian food workers' union [citation:14].

The suit alleged that Coca-Cola and Panamerican Beverages used paramilitaries to kill, torture, and kidnap union leaders at Colombian bottling plants. The specific incident cited was the December 5, 1996 killing of Isidro Segundo Gil, a Sinaltrainal board member shot inside the Carepa bottling plant [citation:14].

The complaint stated that more than 50 union leaders had been killed in Colombia that year, 128 the year before, and more than 1,500 in the past ten years [citation:14].

The case was dismissed. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed in 2009, finding the plaintiffs' allegations of Coca-Cola's control over the bottlers were "too conclusory and lacking sufficient specificity." The legal theory connecting Coca-Cola USA to the paramilitaries was rejected. The killings are not in dispute. The connection to the parent company was.

Sources: BBC News (July 2001), US Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit.

The UN Ruling on Adolfo Múnera

Adolfo Múnera López worked at a Coca-Cola bottling plant in Barranquilla since 1983 and was on the board of Sinaltrainal. He was assassinated on August 31, 2002 [citation:6].

By 2004, nine Sinaltrainal unionists employed by Coca-Cola had been assassinated, 38 workers had fled their cities, and 67 had received death threats [citation:6].

In 2020—eighteen years later—the UN Human Rights Committee ruled that Colombia had violated Múnera's right to life by failing to investigate who ordered the killing, including the potential responsibility of Coca-Cola, which had been publicly accused of links to paramilitary groups [citation:6].

That ruling is public. It is signed. It is the strongest single document in the record.

Source: UN Human Rights Committee (May 2020), ONU-DH Colombia.
▣ IV. THE MYTHOLOGY · KRAMPUS AND SANTA

The Reinvention of Christmas

Before Coca-Cola, Santa Claus was a mixture of folklore: part Saint Nicholas (the charitable bishop) and part Krampus (the dark Alpine figure who punished misbehaving children) [citation:1].

In 1931, Coca-Cola commissioned artist Haddon Sundblom to create a new image of Santa for its advertising. The result—red suit, rosy cheeks, jovial demeanor—matched Coca-Cola's branding perfectly. It became the global symbol of Christmas [citation:1][citation:9].

Coca-Cola didn't just sell beverages. It sold the modern concept of Christmas itself: materialism, indulgence, and endless consumption [citation:1].
