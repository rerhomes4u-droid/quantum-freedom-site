▣ I. THE WATER · LA CALERA, COLOMBIA

They Cemented the Springs

In September 2025, Colombian Congresswoman María del Mar Pizarro denounced that Coca-Cola had covered seven natural springs in La Calera, Cundinamarca, with cement and metal sheets [citation:1][citation:6].

The company claimed they were "protection covers" to prevent contamination. But the regional environmental authority (CAR) confirmed in a field visit that the alterations did not match the company's official reports [citation:1].

Meanwhile, in February 2024, the municipality ordered water rationing because the flow had dropped. The people of La Calera had to buy water for their basic needs—while the Coca-Cola plant continued bottling water for commercial sale [citation:12].

Sources: El Dorado Radio [citation:1], Cuestión Pública [citation:6], La Opinión [citation:12].

▣ II. THE BLOOD · THE UNIONISTS

Isidro Segundo Gil

On December 5, 1996, Isidro Segundo Gil, a board member of the Sinaltrainal union, was shot dead inside the Coca-Cola bottling plant in Carepa, Antioquia [citation:2][citation:13].

Two years later, his wife was also murdered. Their two small children were left orphaned [citation:13].

In 2001, the United Steelworkers and the International Labor Rights Fund sued Coca-Cola and its bottlers in Miami federal court. The complaint stated that more than 50 union leaders had been killed in Colombia that year, 128 the year before, and more than 1,500 in the past ten years [citation:2].

The case was dismissed. The Eleventh Circuit found the plaintiffs' allegations were "too conclusory." The killings are not in dispute. The connection to the parent company was rejected by the court.

Sources: BBC News (2001) [citation:2], CAJAR (2006) [citation:13].

Adolfo Múnera López and the UN Ruling

Adolfo Múnera López worked at a Coca-Cola bottling plant in Barranquilla since 1983 and was on the board of Sinaltrainal. He was assassinated on August 31, 2002.

By 2004, nine Sinaltrainal unionists employed by Coca-Cola had been assassinated. 38 workers had fled their cities. 67 had received death threats.

In 2020—eighteen years later—the UN Human Rights Committee ruled that Colombia had violated Múnera's right to life by failing to investigate who ordered the killing, including the potential responsibility of Coca-Cola.

Source: UN Human Rights Committee (May 2020).
